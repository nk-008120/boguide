Why no finite strategy can ever guarantee a win

Represent the 4 cell states as a bit vector in GF(2)4. A round applies state ← P(state XOR t), where t is the toggle (chosen only from the history of previously observed angles) and P is the permutation matrix for the observed rotation (90°/180°/270° are cyclic shifts, 360° is the identity).

Because the toggle at each round depends only on prior observed angles — never on the invisible state — the entire sequence of toggles for one fixed, already-realized sequence of rotations is fixed in advance, independent of the actual initial state x0. So for that one fixed rotation sequence, the whole D-round process is a single affine map x0 → state_D = M·x0 XOR c, where M is a product of permutation matrices — always invertible.

An invertible map on a 16-element space is a bijection: it sends the 16 distinct initial configurations to 16 distinct final states. It is therefore impossible for all 16 to land on the same target (all "no circle") — at most one of the 16 initial configurations can be solved by any given rotation sequence. Since the adversary (nature) can realize any rotation sequence, no finite strategy can guarantee a win for every configuration.

This holds regardless of which angles are offered (90/180/270/360 or any other rotation set) — only two facts matter: rotations are permutations (hence invertible), and toggles are chosen blind to the state. It also explains why the classic "four blindfolded cups on a spinning table" puzzle is solvable: its goal is "all cups the same" — two target states, not one — so the bijection argument doesn't force a contradiction there.
