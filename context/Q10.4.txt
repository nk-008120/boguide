Q10.4B.  The prediction in Q10.4A rests on a simplification that does not hold up well here. The codon in question translates to methionine. The protein’s local structure still changes substantially despite this substitution. Why? [3]
1)  Methionine, unlike cysteine, is a hydrophobic amino acid, so placing it at this position destabilises the RING domain’s hydrophobic core.
2)  Although both are sulfur-containing, methionine’s thioether sulfur cannot coordinate zinc the way cysteine’s thiol does, and its longer side chain disrupts the tight packing of the zinc-binding cross-brace — so the fold, and function, are still compromised.
3)  Methionine’s side chain is longer than cysteine’s, which increases local flexibility around the zinc-binding loop and thereby further stabilises it.
4)  Since methionine and cysteine are both classified as sulfur-containing amino acids, their side chains are functionally interchangeable, so the fold and function are essentially unaffected.

Q10.4C.  Now suppose the same original residue is instead mutated to serine. How would this affect downstream WNT signalling? [3]
1)  Serine’s near-identical size and shape to cysteine preserve the RING domain fold and zinc coordination, so downstream WNT signalling is unaffected.
2)  Serine cannot coordinate zinc via its hydroxyl group as effectively as cysteine’s thiol, destabilising the RING domain fold, abolishing catalytic activity, and causing WNT hyperactivation (elevated downstream signalling).
3)  Serine’s hydroxyl group forms an even stronger coordination bond with zinc than cysteine’s thiol, enhancing RING domain stability and further suppressing WNT signalling.
4)  Since serine and cysteine are both broadly polar, the substitution has no meaningful structural effect, and downstream signalling proceeds normally via a compensatory pathway.
