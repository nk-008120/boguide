The Biology Olympiad is a competition for secondary school students who enjoy biology and want to go well beyond the school syllabus. Every country runs its own national olympiad, and the best students from each one go on to the **International Biology Olympiad (IBO)**, held in a different country every July. If you have just heard the name for the first time, this page explains how it works and where to start.

## The International Biology Olympiad in brief

The IBO is organised by the International Biology Olympiad association, which has its office in Kiel, Germany. Its stated aim is to find, inspire and support young people who may become the next generation of life scientists, and to connect them with each other across countries.

- **First held:** 1990 in Olomouc, in what was then Czechoslovakia, with 6 countries and 22 students.
- **Today:** about 80 member countries and regions. The 2026 IBO in Vilnius, Lithuania (12 to 19 July) had 301 students from 78 countries.
- **Coming up:** Warsaw, Poland (18 to 25 July 2027), then the Netherlands (2028) and Brno, Czech Republic (2029).
- **Length:** one week, always in July, with exams, ceremonies and time to meet students from other countries.

## Who can take part

You cannot sign up for the IBO yourself. The path always goes through your own country:

1. Your country (or region) must be a member of the IBO and run a **national biology olympiad**.
2. You take part in it while at school. Most national olympiads have several rounds, from a first school or online round to a final selection camp, and each round is harder than the last.
3. The top students are chosen to represent the country. Each member sends **up to four students**, together with up to two jury members.

The official rules also say that competitors must:

- attend a regular secondary school for general education in the country they represent (the location of the school matters, not nationality);
- be no older than 20 on 1 July of the competition year;
- not have started university;
- not have taken part in the IBO twice already.

To find out how your own country runs its olympiad, open the [Country Guides](/biology-olympiad-preparation/). Each guide covers the rounds, dates and who to contact.

## What the exams look like

The IBO has two kinds of exam, and they count equally towards your final score.

**Theoretical exams.** Usually two papers of about three hours each, with roughly 100 tasks in total, mostly closed formats such as multiple true or false. The questions test understanding and the ability to work with data, not memorised facts. The official guidelines give these target proportions:

| Area | Share of the theory |
|---|---|
| Cell biology, including microbiology and biotechnology | 20% |
| Animal anatomy and physiology, with emphasis on vertebrates and humans | 25% |
| Genetics and evolution | 20% |
| Plant anatomy and physiology, with emphasis on seed plants | 15% |
| Ecology | 10% |
| Ethology (animal behaviour) | 5% |
| Biosystematics | 5% |

**Practical exams.** Usually four laboratory sessions of about 90 minutes each. The topics are announced at least a year in advance, and they test hands-on laboratory skills. The official guidelines list the skills that can be assessed.

Questions are often at first-year university level or above, which is why preparation usually starts a year or two ahead.

## How medals are awarded

Results are individual, not by country. Each competitor's theory and practical scores are standardised, weighted equally and combined into a final score. Medals then follow a fixed mathematical procedure, without discussion by the jury:

- **Gold:** roughly the top 10% of competitors
- **Silver:** roughly the next 20%
- **Bronze:** roughly the next 30%
- **Certificate of merit:** roughly the next 10%

Each cutoff is set at the largest score gap near these points, so the exact numbers vary slightly from year to year. Every competitor receives a certificate of participation.

## How to start preparing

1. **Find your national olympiad.** Read your country's page in the [Country Guides](/biology-olympiad-preparation/) for rounds, dates and registration.
2. **Read one topic you already know.** The [Notes](/resources/) follow the IBO areas above. Cell and molecular biology is a good first section.
3. **Try a real question.** [Past Papers](/papers/) has questions from past IBOs and national olympiads, with answers and explanations.
4. **Follow a plan.** The [IBO preparation guide](/ibo-preparation/) sets out a 12-month plan, and there are guides for [USABO](/usabo-preparation/) and [INBO](/inbo-preparation/).
